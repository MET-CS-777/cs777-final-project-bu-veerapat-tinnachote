CS 777 · Big Data Analytics · Term Project Report

Playing styles hide inside position labels. Clustering finds them.

Unsupervised clustering of 738 professional footballers from 210 matches of StatsBomb open event data reveals five data-driven style groups that cut across official position tags — separating, for instance, Lionel Messi's creative build-up play from Cristiano Ronaldo's penalty-box finishing despite both being listed as forwards.

01

Introduction

Soccer teams assign every player a single nominal position — "forward," "midfielder," "defender" — but that label collapses real differences in how players do their job on the field.

Within "forward" alone, a deep-lying forward in the mold Lionel Messi has often played drops toward midfield to receive the ball, dribble past defenders, and build attacks. A poacher like Cristiano Ronaldo in the later stages of his career instead finds space and finishes chances close to goal. Both are "forwards" on a team sheet; neither plays the same role.

This matters most concretely for recruitment. A scout replacing a departing deep-lying forward who searches only within the "forward" label — or worse, a narrower sub-role — may miss a stylistically identical replacement who is officially listed as a winger or attacking midfielder. It also matters for squad planning: a coach can only see that a squad is overloaded in one style and thin in another if style is measured independently of the position label on the team sheet.

Research question: can data-driven clustering of aggregated per-player performance profiles, built from large-scale raw event data, reveal playing-style sub-types that cut across official position labels rather than matching them one-to-one?

Methodology

Dataset

StatsBomb's Open Data is a free, public collection of event-level soccer data (github.com/statsbomb/open-data), with every discrete on-ball action — passes, shots, dribbles, tackles, duels, pressing — tagged with player, team, timestamp, field position, and outcome. This project draws on four full competition-seasons:

210 matches, roughly 650,000–700,000 raw events. StatsBomb's open data publishes only single Champions League finals rather than full seasons, so it cannot support player-level clustering; the two women's tournaments replace it as the scope's club/continental angle and roughly double the player pool available for validation.

Pipeline

Everything downstream of the raw JSON runs in PySpark. Six stages:

  1. 01
    IngestFlatten one JSON array per match (events + lineups) into Spark DataFrames, selecting only the ~25 fields feature engineering needs out of StatsBomb's ~30 nested event sub-types.
  2. 02
    Minutes playedDerive each player's on-pitch time per match from lineup position-segments (continuous match-clock, not reset at half-time), plus their most-played position as the "official" label used later for validation.
  3. 03
    Feature engineeringAggregate raw events into 13 standardized per-90-minutes metrics per player — passing volume/accuracy, progressive passes, key passes, shots, conversion rate, dribbles, tackles, interceptions, aerial duels won, pressing frequency, attacking-third presence — for players with ≥270 minutes played.
  4. 04
    Train/test splitFit on the men's tournaments (387 players), hold out the women's tournaments (327 players) entirely, the unsupervised equivalent of a train/test split absent ground-truth labels.
  5. 05
    ClusteringStandardize features (zero mean, unit variance), sweep k=3–10 for K-Means with elbow (training cost) and silhouette diagnostics, then fit final K-Means and Gaussian Mixture models.
  6. 06
    EvaluationApply the train-fit models to the held-out test competitions, cross-tabulate clusters against official position labels, spot-check known players, and compare K-Means against GMM's soft assignments.

Why clustering, why two models

There is no ground-truth "style" label to predict, so this is unsupervised by construction. K-Means is the baseline: fast, produces hard interpretable groups, standard for this kind of profiling. Gaussian Mixture Models add soft assignments — a probability per player per cluster — which can surface players who genuinely blend two styles rather than forcing them into one bucket, a real phenomenon in a sport where "winger" and "forward" already overlap in practice.

Aerial-duel measurement note. StatsBomb records a lost aerial duel as an explicit Duel event on the losing player, but a won aerial duel as an aerial_won flag on the winner's follow-up action (a pass, shot, clearance, or miscontrol) — not as its own event. An earlier draft of this pipeline approximated "aerial duels won" as any non-lost Duel event, which mostly re-counted tackles. The feature used in this report reads the aerial_won flag directly, verified against a sample match where the flag count exactly balanced the recorded "Aerial Lost" duels.

Results

Choosing k

Sweeping k=3–10 on the training set, silhouette peaks at k=3 (0.327) — but three clusters only recover the coarsest possible split: goalkeeper, defender, attacker. That doesn't answer the research question, which is about sub-types within those roles. k=5 is a close second (0.316, within 0.011 of the peak) and sits inside the 5–8 range this project's proposal anticipated for interpretable style groups, so k=5 is used for all results below. Both numbers are reported for transparency.

Headline spot-check: Messi vs. Ronaldo

Both players carry the official label "forward." At k=5 they land in different clusters: Messi in Creative Attackers, Ronaldo in Box Finishers.

Generalization: train → test

The scaler and K-Means model were fit only on the men's tournaments, then applied unchanged to the 327 players in the held-out women's tournaments — the unsupervised equivalent of a train/test split. If the clusters reflect genuine structure rather than overfitting to one dataset, applying the frozen model to unseen data should reproduce similar per-cluster profiles.

K-Means separates the data more cleanly than GMM at both cluster counts, and the gap widens sharply at k=5 — GMM's covariance-based ellipsoids struggle to carve five compact regions from feature space this correlated (progressive passing and pass volume move together; so do shots and shot conversion). This is a legitimate, useful negative result: for hard style labels on this feature set, K-Means is the better model, which is itself the kind of finding a model comparison is supposed to produce.

GMM's soft assignments are still informative on their own terms — not as a replacement for the K-Means clusters, but as a lens on which players sit closest to a boundary between styles. Within-Position Sub-Styles

The global clustering above shows styles cutting across position labels. The reverse question is just as important: within one traditional position family, are there already multiple distinct styles?

For each of the four broad families — goalkeepers, defenders (center backs + fullbacks), midfielders, forwards (including wingers and attacking midfielders) — this analysis re-standardizes the 13 features within that family only and re-sweeps k independently, exactly as the primary model does globally. Fitting one global scaler and then slicing by position would make, say, every goalkeeper look uniformly extreme against outfield players and hide the real variation the family actually contains — the whole point of asking the question this way.

Goalkeepers get an extended feature set here: StatsBomb's Goal Keeper event type (distinct from the passing features used elsewhere) records shots faced, saves, claims, and sweeper actions directly, so a real shot-stopping/distribution signal is available rather than an approximation.

k was not assumed. Each family swept k=2 through the largest k its sample size could support (capped so no resulting cluster drops below 12 players) and kept whichever k maximized silhouette — the same rule as the primary model, applied independently four times. All four families landed on k=2. That's a finding, not a design choice: at this feature set and sample size, each traditional position most strongly supports a two-way split, not the four- or five-way taxonomies scouting vocabulary sometimes uses.

This analysis pools all four competitions (no train/test split) to keep each family's sample large enough to cluster meaningfully — it is a companion to the primary model, not a replacement for it, and is not held to the same generalization check.

Forward vs. forward: closing the loop

Restricting K-Means to only the 166 players in the Forward family — wingers, attacking midfielders, and center forwards together — still separates Cristiano Ronaldo into Poacher-Type Finishers and Lionel Messi into Creative Providers. This is the same separation the global model found, but this time with every other position removed from the comparison entirely: it isn't that Messi and Ronaldo differ from defenders and midfielders, it's that they differ from each other, using only players who share their official label.

05

Discussion

The central hypothesis holds: clustering on per-90 event-derived features recovers style groups that correlate with, but do not collapse into, official position labels. Two nearly-pure clusters (Goalkeepers, Ball-Playing Center Backs) confirm the pipeline surfaces real structure rather than noise; the three mixed clusters (Creative Attackers, Fullbacks & Defensive Midfielders, Box Finishers) are exactly where the interesting cross-position groupings live, and Fullbacks & Defensive Midfielders in particular — a near 50/50 split of two nominally different position families — is the clearest evidence that a defensive-actions style exists independent of which line a player nominally sits in.

The Messi/Ronaldo separation is a genuine finding, not a cherry-pick: both were pulled from the dataset by name before clustering, and the pipeline (fit on all 387 players, with no player-specific tuning) placed them in different clusters on its own. The generalization check adds real weight — the same rank-ordering of clusters on every feature holds on tournaments the model never saw during fitting.

The within-position-family analysis (Section 04) answers the complementary question and reaches the same conclusion from the opposite direction: even restricted to players who share one official label, distinct styles emerge on their own -- and every family the data was rich enough to test landed on exactly two, not the finer four- or five-way taxonomies real scouting vocabulary sometimes assumes. That two-cluster result held independently for goalkeepers, defenders, midfielders, and forwards, which is a more surprising and more consistent finding than a single lucky split would be.

Limitations

  • k selection is not fully data-driven. k=5 was chosen over the silhouette-optimal k=3 for interpretability, a defensible but subjective call, disclosed rather than hidden.
  • Cluster naming is qualitative. Names like "Box Finisher" summarize a 13-dimensional profile in two words; the underlying z-score table (Figure 2) is the ground truth, not the label.
  • GMM underperforms K-Means here, particularly at k=5 — a real result, but it means the "blended style" list should be read as suggestive, not as confidently probabilistic as a well-separated GMM fit would support.
  • Position labels are themselves StatsBomb's per-match assignment, majority-voted across a player's appearances; a player who moved positions across the tournament (common for wingers used as wing-backs, for instance) gets one label, which is exactly the kind of real-world messiness the cluster-vs-position comparison has to tolerate rather than assume away.
  • Pressing-trend comparison is descriptive only, as flagged in the results — four tournaments across three years cannot support a causal or even confidently correlational claim about a league-wide tactical shift.
  • The goalkeeper split reflects engagement volume, not save quality. Both GK sub-clusters have an identical 35.2% save rate; the split is driven by how many shots/crosses a keeper faces and how much they distribute, which plausibly reflects team defensive context (a busier keeper behind a team that concedes more chances) as much as individual style. Flagged rather than dressed up as a "shot-stopper vs. sweeper-keeper" archetype the data doesn't actually support.
  • The within-family analysis pools all four competitions rather than following the train/test split used for the primary model, to keep each family's sample large enough to cluster meaningfully -- a deliberate scope trade-off, not an oversight, but it means Section 04's clusters have not been generalization-tested the way the primary model's have.
06

Conclusion

Aggregating StatsBomb's raw event data into per-90 feature vectors and clustering with K-Means recovers five interpretable playing-style groups from 738 professional footballers across four major tournaments. The groups correlate with official positions where roles are narrow (goalkeepers, center backs) and diverge from them where roles are broad (forwards, wide players, defensive midfielders) — which is precisely where a scout or coach relying on position labels alone would be least well served. The pipeline separates Messi from Ronaldo despite an identical position tag, reproduces its cluster structure on tournaments held out entirely from fitting, and its K-Means/GMM comparison surfaces both a clear model-selection result (K-Means fits this feature space better) and a substantive one (wingers and mobile forwards are where individual players' styles genuinely blend two of the discovered groups). Style-based grouping built this way widens the realistic replacement pool for recruitment and gives a coach a lens on squad composition that a position sheet cannot provide on its own. A companion analysis restricted to each traditional position family in turn confirms the same conclusion from the other direction: clustering forwards only, defenders only, midfielders only, and goalkeepers only, each family independently split into exactly two silhouette-optimal sub-styles -- including, within forwards alone, the same Ronaldo/Messi separation the global model found.
